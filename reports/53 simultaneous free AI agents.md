# Split one GPU into 53 brains

The best place to get 53 AI "brains" thinking at once for $0 is your own RTX 3060. The free llama.cpp server can load one small model a single time and give it 53 "slots". Each slot holds its own separate conversation, and there is no daily cap. The catch is size and speed. Only very small "hybrid" models leave room for 53 conversations of about 4,000 tokens each in the card's roughly 5 GB of usable memory: **Qwen3.5-2B (about 4.3 GB in total), LFM2.5-1.2B (about 2.6 GB) and LFM2-2.6B (about 4.0 GB)**. The smarter Qwen3.5-4B would need about 9.5 GB, so it gets about 20 slots instead. The 53 brains also share one chip's speed. Each writes an estimated 17–40 tokens a second, and when all 53 read a fresh page at once, each waits 30–60 seconds for its answer. The group as a whole gets only **about 1.5–2.5 times more done than one brain working alone, or about 1–1.7 page-steps a second**. Free hosted AI services can take a one-off burst of 53 requests. Combined, though, they keep 53 agents going (one step every 15 seconds each) for **only about 2–3 hours on the most hopeful Gemini figures, and for minutes on the worst**. After that they support 26–37 agents. Kaggle's free pair of T4 graphics cards can run 53 slots of a smarter 4B model, but only for about 30 hours a week. rtrvr's own "53 agents" video did not show 53 independent thinkers. It ran **one extraction prompt across 105 Product Hunt pages on 53 cloud browsers in 184 seconds**, with Gemini Flash on Google Cloud doing the thinking. The recommended $0 build copies that shape. One smarter free cloud model plans the job. 53 local slots each read one page and fill in a tiny fixed form. Ordinary code does the loading and clicking. An AI-written "recipe" replaces the AI entirely when pages share a layout. On our estimates, this matches the demo's roughly three minutes for 105 pages, on your laptop.

All figures are as of October 18, 2026. Throughout, "estimated" marks numbers worked out from published specifications rather than measured on your machine. No one has published a benchmark for an RTX 3060 Laptop running dozens of conversations at once.

## rtrvr's "53 agents" ran one prompt over 105 pages

rtrvr's homepage advertises the run as "One question, researched 53 ways at once" ([rtrvr.ai](https://rtrvr.ai/)). The video behind it, "I Ran 53 AI Agents at Once. Here's What Happened.", was posted by rtrvr's own channel on January 4, 2026. Its description sums it up: **"53 cloud browsers. 105 Product Hunt companies. 184 seconds."** The user picks a spreadsheet of Product Hunt links and gives a single instruction: "Extract company name, description, launches, reviews, rating, followers, team members, pros and cons." The work is then spread across 53 browsers in rtrvr's cloud ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ggLDvZKuBlU)). The video shows a live grid of the browsers and lets a human take over mid-task. It also advertises built-in captcha solving and has a segment on scaling to 150+ browsers. It never names the AI model. rtrvr's Google Cloud page fills that gap: **Gemini Flash handles the "reasoning + tools"**, Google Cloud Run runs the isolated browsers, and the whole stack is "100% GCP" ([rtrvr Google Cloud page](https://rtrvr.ai/google-cloud-partner)).

Do the arithmetic and the demo shrinks. 105 pages over 53 browsers is about two pages per browser. 184 seconds in total means roughly 90 seconds per page, browser start-up included. No agent was planning anything. Every agent got the same instruction and the same kind of page, and filled in the same form. This is a "map" job: one question applied to a list. That is good news for a free copy. Filling a fixed form from one page is the job small free models do best, and it does not need 53 separate minds running at full speed. It needs enough combined reading power to get through 105 pages in about three minutes. Two parts of the demo are worth not copying. Captcha solving collides with sites' terms and anti-bot rules. And instead of paid cloud browsers, you use your own Chrome and home connection.

## One model with 53 slots runs all day, but the slots share one engine

The free llama.cpp server (`llama-server`) can load a model once and serve many conversations at the same time. The `-np 53` setting creates 53 slots, each holding its own separate conversation. "Continuous batching", which lets new requests join while others are still running, is on by default ([llama.cpp server README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md)). The same page documents three memory-saving settings this plan needs. The first is one shared memory pool for all slots (`-kvu`), with a cap per slot (`--kv-unified-per-slot`). The second is compressed memory (`q8_0`, about half the size). The third is "flash attention", a faster way of computing. Each conversation needs short-term memory that grows with every token the model reads. (A token is about three-quarters of a word.) That memory, not the model itself, decides how many slots fit on your card.

That rules out most small models. An ordinary design like Qwen3-1.7B would need an estimated 12 GB of conversation memory for 53 pages of 4,000 tokens, even when compressed. That is twice your whole card. What works is a newer "hybrid" design, in which most layers keep a small fixed-size summary instead of a growing memory. In Qwen3.5-2B, only 6 of 24 layers keep the growing kind, with just 2 memory "heads" each ([Qwen3.5-2B config](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B/raw/main/config.json)). Liquid AI's LFM2.5-1.2B has 6 such layers out of 16 ([LFM2.5-1.2B config](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct/raw/main/config.json)). Plan on about 5–5.3 GB of the 6 GB being usable. One user with a 6 GB card under WSL2 (Linux running inside Windows) saw about 4.99 GB available ([dev.to](https://dev.to/lugerlogic/running-tiny-llms-on-your-local-machine-with-vllm-wsl2-no-cloud-needed-5c44)).

| Model | Estimated total memory for 53 conversations of 4,000 tokens | 53 slots on your card? |
|---|---|---|
| LFM2.5-1.2B | about 2.6 GB | Yes, easily |
| Qwen3.5-0.8B | about 3.6 GB | Yes |
| LFM2-2.6B | about 4.0 GB | Yes |
| Qwen3.5-2B | about 4.3 GB | Yes; use stronger compression (`q4_0`) for 6,000-token pages |
| Qwen3.5-4B | about 9.5 GB | No; about 20 slots |
| Ordinary small models (e.g. Qwen3-1.7B) | 12 GB or more for conversation memory alone | No; about 15 slots |

The totals combine model size, compressed conversation memory and working overhead. They are estimated from the published designs of [Qwen3.5-0.8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B/raw/main/config.json), [Qwen3.5-2B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B/raw/main/config.json), [Qwen3.5-4B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B/raw/main/config.json), [LFM2.5-1.2B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct/raw/main/config.json) and [LFM2-2.6B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-2.6B/raw/main/config.json), with compression halving memory as the [Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq) describes. The Qwen3-1.7B row uses an assumed design. Qwen3.5's fixed summaries are not free. Each conversation carries about 19 MB of them, about 1 GB for 53. Qwen3.5-4B needs about 50 MB each, which helps push it over the limit.

### Reading doesn't speed up with more slots; writing does

Every agent step has two parts. First the model reads the prompt, mostly the page. Then it writes a short answer. Reading is limited by the chip's raw arithmetic, so batching does not speed it up. llama.cpp's own benchmark example reads at **1,187 tokens a second with one conversation and 1,202 with 32**. Writing behaves differently. In the same example it rose from **41.6 tokens a second for one conversation to 465 for 32 combined**, about 11 times more, even though each conversation slowed to about 14.5 tokens a second ([batched-bench README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/batched-bench/README.md)).

Your laptop's numbers have to be estimated. A desktop RTX 3060 reads 2,408 tokens a second on a 7B model ([llama.cpp scoreboard](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15013)). Scaled to smaller models, your laptop should read roughly **4,000–8,000 tokens a second with Qwen3.5-2B and 6,000–12,000 with LFM2.5-1.2B**. In one full "round", all 53 agents send a 4,000-token page, about 217,000 tokens of reading. That takes an estimated 27–54 seconds on Qwen3.5-2B, or 18–36 seconds on LFM2.5-1.2B. The time is the same whether the requests run together or one by one. Writing a 100-token answer adds 3–6 seconds.

So, per brain: each of the 53 writes an estimated **17–40 tokens a second**. When they all ask at once, **each waits about 30–60 seconds per step**, against about 1.5 seconds for a brain working alone. In total, the group finishes roughly **1–1.7 steps a second, or about 3,600–6,000 single-page reads an hour**. That is only 1.5–2.5 times what the same model does one request at a time. Running 53 slots is not 53 times faster; it mainly keeps the chip busy. It can also run as long as the laptop is on. There is no daily cap and no account, and nothing leaves your machine. Laptop power limits and heat will move these numbers, so measure your own.

The biggest lever is less reading, not more slots. **Halving the page snapshot from 4,000 to 2,000 tokens halves the round time.** Reusing a shared instruction block saves at most about a quarter, because the page itself changes every step.

### Windows, memory traps and the browser side

llama.cpp is the right engine on Windows. vLLM, the usual choice for big batched servers, "does not support Windows natively" ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/getting_started/installation/gpu.html)). On a 6 GB card under WSL2, one user got only a 0.6B model running, one conversation at a time ([dev.to](https://dev.to/lugerlogic/running-tiny-llms-on-your-local-machine-with-vllm-wsl2-no-cloud-needed-5c44)). Ollama can run parallel requests through `OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL`, which defaults to 1, with memory growing to match ([Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq)). But it hides the per-slot caps and other controls that make 53 slots fit. Two llama-server defaults quietly eat the RAM your browsers need: up to **32 "checkpoints" per slot, and an 8,192 MB prompt cache** in system memory ([llama.cpp server README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md)). With Qwen3.5's 19 MB per conversation, 32 checkpoints × 53 slots could reach about 32 GB in the worst case, twice your RAM. This assumes the checkpoints sit in system memory, which is unconfirmed. Turn both settings down. Set the slot count explicitly too, because defaults have shifted between builds ([llama.cpp issue #17989](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/17989)). Qwen3.5's small models also think out loud by default ([Qwen3.5-4B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B)). Switch thinking off so each answer stays near 100 tokens.

53 brains don't need 53 tabs loading at once. A loading tab takes about 150–500 MB ([webscraping.ai](https://webscraping.ai/faq/headless-chromium/what-are-the-resource-requirements-for-running-headless-chromium-at-scale)), and six CPU cores should handle an estimated 12–20 at once. Each slot spends most of a round waiting for the shared chip, so 12–20 active tabs can keep 53 slots fed. Your Chrome extension can call the local server from its background service worker, as long as it declares host permission for localhost ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/develop/concepts/network-requests)). The Intel graphics chip and the CPU aren't worth adding as extra brains. They share memory bandwidth with Chrome, and the research found no evidence that they would add meaningful speed.

## Free cloud APIs give a 53-wide burst, not a 53-wide day

The free hosted services look like a fleet of brains, but one you only get for a few hours. Assume 53 agents, each sending about 5,000 tokens and getting about 100 back per step. If each agent steps every 15 seconds, that is **212 requests a minute**. Every 5 seconds, it is 636.

Running 53 requests at the same moment is not the problem. Almost no free provider publishes a cap on open requests; they cap requests per minute and per day. The exceptions are Z AI's GLM-Flash models, at one request at a time per model ([mnfst tracker](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis)), and Mistral's reported one request per second ([Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/MistralAI/comments/1rc8rwf/mistral_api_quota_and_rate_limits_pools_analysis/)). Cloudflare allows 300 text requests a minute and lists no concurrency limit ([Cloudflare limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/limits/)), so a one-off burst of 53 is easy. In practice, at an assumed 3 seconds per answer, 53 agents keep only about 11–32 requests open at a time on average. The real limits are how many requests you get each minute and each day.

| Free provider | Requests a minute it can sustain | Daily cap and how long it lasts at that pace | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Gemini (about 8 free models) | 80–120 | 160, 2,000 or 12,000 a day: minutes to about 2.5 hours | Community figures that conflict |
| Mistral (one key) | 30–60 | No daily cap; about 1 billion tokens a month | Community |
| NVIDIA | 40 | Possibly 10,000 a day per model: about 4 hours | Rate confirmed on NVIDIA's forum; daily cap unconfirmed |
| OVH (no key, per internet address) | about 22 | None published | Community |
| Z AI GLM-Flash | about 12–24 | Unpublished | Community, plus a latency guess |
| Kilo gateway | about 3 | 200 an hour | Community |
| Groq | about 3 | About 117 a day: about 40 minutes | Official |
| Cloudflare | Burst up to 300 | About 380 a day: about 1.3 minutes at full speed | Official |
| OpenRouter free models | 20 | 50 a day: 2.5 minutes | Official |

Sources: [Gemini rate limits](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits), [klymentiev](https://klymentiev.com/blog/gemini-api-free-tier), [pricepertoken](https://pricepertoken.com/endpoints/mistral/free), [NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/request-for-nvidia-build-api-rate-limit-increase-40-rpm-200-rpm-personal-agentic-ai-development/373571), [mnfst tracker](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis), [Groq](https://console.groq.com/docs/rate-limits), [Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/pricing/), [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api-reference/limits). "How long it lasts" means at that provider's full pace. Groq's and Cloudflare's daily caps count tokens, so their request numbers assume about 5,100 tokens per request.

While Gemini's daily allowance lasts, these services together sustain **about 190–272 requests a minute**. That is enough for 47–68 agents stepping every 15 seconds, or 15–22 stepping every 5 seconds. How long 53 agents last depends on Gemini's daily cap, which nobody can pin down. Google shows exact free limits only inside each account and warns that "Specified rate limits are not guaranteed" ([Gemini rate limits](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits)). October 2026 sources range from 1,500 requests a day per Flash model ([klymentiev](https://klymentiev.com/blog/gemini-api-free-tier)), to 250 ([Gemini CLI docs](https://geminicli.com/docs/resources/quota-and-pricing/)), to 20 ([aifreeapi](https://www.aifreeapi.com/en/posts/google-gemini-api-free-tier)). Users also reported free-tier errors and stuck limits in mid-October ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/tag/rate-limits/415)). At 1,500 a day, 53 agents run for **about 2–3 hours**. At 250, about 20–30 minutes. At 20, about 2 minutes. After Gemini runs out, the rest sustain 107–149 requests a minute, or **26–37 agents at the 15-second pace**. Once every capped service is used up, the services with no known daily cap (OVH, Z AI, Kilo) support only 9–12 agents. Across a full day, all the free supply covers 23–75% of what 53 slow agents need. Mistral's pool, reported at about a billion tokens a month, is the biggest. At this pace it would be gone in about 2–4.5 days ([mistral-vibe issue](https://github.com/mistralai/mistral-vibe/issues/275)).

Three more catches. Only Groq officially confirms tool calling ([Groq tool use](https://console.groq.com/docs/tool-use)). Ask every model for plain JSON and check the reply in code. Extra accounts don't multiply quota either. OpenRouter says more accounts or keys "will not affect your rate limits" ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api-reference/limits)), and NVIDIA says there is "no official way to circumvent this rate limit" ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/request-for-nvidia-build-api-rate-limit-increase-40-rpm-200-rpm-personal-agentic-ai-development/373571)). Spreading work across *different* providers is ordinary fallback, and nothing the research found forbids it. And the free tiers aren't private. Google's terms say "Do not submit sensitive, confidential, or personal information to the Unpaid Services" ([Gemini terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). The volunteer-run AI Horde is no escape hatch. On October 18, its whole network produced about 19,600 tokens a minute ([AI Horde](https://aihorde.net/api/v2/status/performance)). 53 agents stepping every 10 seconds would need about 31,800 tokens a minute of answers alone. Two former favourites are gone: Chutes ended its free allowance ([Chutes](https://chutes.ai/news/community-announcement-february)), and GitHub Models was retired on July 30, 2026 ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-01-github-models-is-being-fully-retired-on-july-30-2026/)).

## Kaggle's free T4 pair buys a smarter brain for 30 hours a week

Kaggle is the one free graphics-card host worth using for this. Its free notebooks offer two Nvidia T4 cards with 16 GB each, or one older P100, in sessions of up to 12 hours ([Kaggle Notebooks docs](https://www.kaggle.com/docs/notebooks)). The weekly GPU allowance "is 30 hours or sometimes higher depending on demand and resources" ([Kaggle GPU docs](https://www.kaggle.com/docs/efficient-gpu-usage)). Both options draw on the same allowance, so always pick the pair ([Kaggle announcement](https://www.kaggle.com/product-feedback/361104)). Community and course guides say you need a verified phone number to turn on internet access and GPUs ([fast.ai forums](https://forums.fast.ai/t/beginner-using-colab-or-kaggle/96280)). Kaggle's terms allow one account per person and only "your own internal, personal, non-commercial use" ([Kaggle Terms](https://www.kaggle.com/terms)). They say nothing either way about running a server or a tunnel. That leaves serving your own extension in a grey zone. Serving other people, or a product, would break the rules.

The pair has 32 GB between them, more than five times what your laptop card can use, so they can hold a smarter 4B model across all 53 slots. A community toolkit, kaggle-vllm, has confirmed that vLLM runs across both T4s ([kaggle-vllm](https://pypi.org/project/kaggle-vllm/)). Here is the research's estimate for a compressed 4B model such as Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. Each of 53 conversations gets room for about 3,000 tokens. Each agent writes **about 8–12 tokens a second**. In total the pair does **roughly 30–100 agent steps a minute**. That is about your laptop's total speed, but with a smarter model. No one has published a real 53-conversation test on T4s, so treat these numbers as rough. Qwen3 was trained in a number format called bfloat16 ([Qwen3-4B config](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B/raw/main/config.json)), which the T4 lacks, so it has to run in the older float16 format instead ([RunAIHome](https://runaihome.com/blog/flashattention-only-supports-ampere-gpus-fix-2026/)). That can break some models' answers, so test the exact model before trusting it.

Uptime is the real limit. One account gives **about 30 hours a week, an average of about 4.3 hours a day**, in sessions of up to 12 hours. Running the notebook as a committed background job ("Save & Run All") should keep it alive after you close the browser. No source has tested a server this way. To reach the notebook from your laptop, use a free Cloudflare Quick Tunnel. It needs no account and "supports up to 200 in-flight requests", which is enough for 53. But it does not support streamed replies and is meant "for testing and development" ([Cloudflare Quick Tunnels](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/networks/connectors/cloudflare-tunnel/do-more-with-tunnels/trycloudflare/)). Have the extension ask for whole answers, and paste in the new tunnel address each session. ngrok's free plan caps you at 20,000 requests a month ([ngrok](https://ngrok.com/docs/pricing-limits/free-plan-limits/)), which 53 agents would use up in about six hours.

Every other free GPU route fails for one reason or another. Google Colab's free tier bans "web service offerings not related to interactive compute", "connecting to remote proxies", and, for free users, "bypassing the notebook UI" ([Colab FAQ](https://research.google.com/colaboratory/faq.html)). Lightning AI has been offline to all users since October 16, 2026, after a security incident ([Lightning AI](https://lightning.ai/)). Amazon's SageMaker Studio Lab is closed to new users, Hugging Face's ZeroGPU gives minutes a day, and Google Cloud's trial doesn't allow GPUs ([GPUPerHour](https://gpuperhour.com/blog/free-cloud-gpus-and-credits)). Modal gives "$30 / month free credits", about 51 T4-hours, with ready-made web addresses and no tunnel needed. But its page doesn't say whether it needs a card ([Modal pricing](https://modal.com/pricing)). AMD offers a one-off $100 credit by application, which expires after 30 days ([AMD Developer Cloud](https://www.amd.com/en/developer/resources/cloud-access/amd-developer-cloud.html)).

## Small brains succeed when the job is one page and one small form

The models that fit 53 slots on your card are not the strongest small models. Tool-calling scores show the trade-off. They come from BFCL-V4, a test of whether a model calls tools correctly.

| Model | BFCL-V4 tool calling | TAU2 (multi-step tasks) | 53 slots on your laptop? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen3.5-9B | 66.1 | 79.1 | No; the model alone is a 6.6–7.6 GB download |
| LFM2.5-2.6B | 56.9 | not found | Probably (about 4 GB, judging by LFM2-2.6B's design) |
| Granite 4.2 3B | 52.4 | not found | Not checked |
| Qwen3.5-4B | 50.3 | 79.9 | No; about 20 slots |
| Qwen3.5-2B | 43.6 | 48.8 | Yes |
| Qwen3.5-0.8B | 25.3 | 11.6 | Yes |

Sources: [llm-stats BFCL-V4](https://llm-stats.com/benchmarks/bfcl-v4), [Qwen3.5-4B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B), [Qwen3.5-2B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B), [Qwen3.5-0.8B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B), [Ollama library](https://ollama.com/library/qwen3.5).

Every score in that table is self-reported by the model makers. The aggregator lists **0 of 21 BFCL-V4 scores as verified**, and the official leaderboard was last updated on April 12, 2026 ([BFCL](https://gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu/leaderboard.html)). The Qwen3.5 small models are Apache 2.0, free even for commercial use ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260303-qwen-3-5-small)). Salesforce's strong xLAM-2 tool models are licensed "for research purposes only" ([xLAM-2 card](https://huggingface.co/Salesforce/Llama-xLAM-2-8b-fc-r)). For a 53-slot laptop, the practical picks are these. **Qwen3.5-2B** is the default. **LFM2.5-1.2B** is the light, fast option. **LFM2.5-2.6B** is worth testing, because it has the best self-reported tool score among models that probably fit. Its memory was estimated from the older LFM2-2.6B design, and its licence should be checked. Keep Qwen3.5-0.8B for simple sorting jobs. On Kaggle, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 scores 61.9 on the older BFCL-v3 and skips the long "thinking" text ([Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507)).

Small models are weak at browsing on their own. A general 7B model completed 8.3% of WebArena tasks, against 21.7% for a 7B trained on web exploration ([Go-Browse](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.03533)). Training changes the picture: a 9B student taught by Gemini 3 Pro reached **41.5% on WebArena, beating GPT-4o's 31.5%** ([Agent-as-Annotators](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07776)). Without such training, give workers narrow jobs, not open-ended ones.

Forcing the output into a fixed JSON shape helps and hurts. It guarantees answers your code can read. Validity rose from 78.6–92.9% to 100% across five models from 0.6B to 4B, but mistakes in the content remained ([Chavan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23742)). Forcing the shape from the very first word can make tiny models worse. One study found Qwen2.5-1.5B's correct tool calls **fell from 91.5% to 48.0%** under a strict schema, though both versions were 100% well-formed ([The Constraint Tax](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26128)). That study's fix is "reason free, constrain late". Let the worker write a sentence of reasoning, then force only the final JSON. Use temperature 0, and cap the length.

The designs with the best evidence pair one capable planner with many narrow workers. The MAKER project completed a task of about **one million AI steps with zero errors**. It split the work into tiny steps, had several samples vote on each step, and threw out answers that ran long or broke format. It found "relatively small non-reasoning models" good enough per step. It also found that error rates "rise sharply" once answers pass about 700 tokens, and that llama-3.2-3B failed every single step ([MAKER](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030)). So test each model on each kind of job before relying on it. Voting gains grow with the number of voters ([More Agents Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05120)), but three votes for 53 agents means 159 answers, so vote only where answers disagree or a field matters. Splitting a web agent into a planner and a doer reached 57.58% on WebArena-Lite ([Plan-and-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09572v3)). NVIDIA's researchers argue that agent systems should use small models for repetitive calls and keep a larger model only for planning ([Belcak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02153)). rtrvr's own documentation describes a planner directing "20+ specialized sub-agents" ([rtrvr docs](https://www.rtrvr.ai/docs/web-agent)).

## A $0 blueprint that matches the demo's three minutes

Put the pieces side by side and each free option has a clear role.

| Where the brains live | Brain quality | Speed per brain with 53 running | Total work | How long 53 last | Privacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Your laptop, Qwen3.5-2B, 53 slots | Small (tool score 43.6) | ~17–40 tokens/s; 30–60 s wait per step | ~1–1.7 steps/s | All day, every day | Never leaves the laptop |
| Kaggle 2× T4, 4B model, 53 slots | Better 4B class | ~8–12 tokens/s | ~30–100 steps/min | Up to 12 h a session, ~30 h a week | Google's machine; personal use only |
| Free cloud APIs combined | Large cloud models | Not measured (~3 s assumed) | 190–272 requests/min, then 107–149 | ~2–3 h best case; minutes worst case | Public pages only |
| rtrvr's demo (for comparison) | Gemini Flash, paid cloud | ~90 s per page job | 105 pages in 184 s | Paid | rtrvr's cloud |

All figures except rtrvr's are estimates, from the sections above.

The recommended build is a planner, a no-AI fast lane, 53 local workers and a cloud overflow. **The planner** is one free cloud model, such as Gemini Flash, Mistral or Groq's gpt-oss-120b. It turns your prompt into a job list (each job a link plus a small form) and merges the results at the end. That is a handful of calls per job, well inside the free limits. Use the local model instead when the job touches signed-in pages. **The fast lane** runs before any worker. It reads the structured data that about 51% of web pages already carry ([Web Data Commons](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/dws/news/wdc-json-ld-microdata-rdfa-data-corpus-2024-published)). Then it uses Crawl4AI to have an AI write a recipe for each page layout. Crawl4AI calls this "a one-time cost" that "can be reused for unlimited extractions without further LLM calls" ([Crawl4AI](https://docs.crawl4ai.com/extraction/no-llm-strategies/)). **The workers** are the 53 llama-server slots. Each reads one trimmed page of about 2,000 tokens, writes a sentence of reasoning, and fills in the form. Code checks the result, asks again once on failure, and calls a vote only where answers disagree. **The browsers** are one Chrome with 12–20 pages loading at a time and the rest queued. They stay polite: one request per site at a time, with 1–3 second pauses, which matches Crawl4AI's default limiter ([Crawl4AI](https://docs.crawl4ai.com/advanced/multi-url-crawling/)). **The overflow** sends hard public pages to the free cloud APIs, spread across providers, backing off when told to slow down. Private pages always stay local. A Kaggle session covers the occasional big day when you want 53 smarter brains.

Now apply this to the demo's job. Suppose the AI has to read all 105 Product Hunt pages, trimmed to about 2,000 tokens each. That is about 210,000 tokens of reading: an estimated 25–55 seconds on Qwen3.5-2B, plus a few seconds of writing. At the full 4,000 tokens per page, it is about 1–2 minutes. A browser loads about 2.5 pages a second per browser process ([MarkCrawl benchmark](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/AIMLPM/markcrawl/main/docs/BENCHMARKS.md)), so loading 105 pages takes under a minute and overlaps with the reading. That puts the whole job at **an estimated 1–3 minutes, against rtrvr's 184 seconds**, with no cloud and no ads. All 105 pages share Product Hunt's layout, so fields that sit in the same place on every page need only one recipe call and a replay. Scaled up to "1,000 sites, one prompt", the same worker pool needs roughly 10–17 minutes of AI time for one-read-per-page jobs.

Start like this. Confirm the 6 GB with `nvidia-smi`. Then run llama.cpp's own test with each candidate model, which replaces these estimates with real numbers in minutes:

```
llama-batched-bench -m <model>.gguf -ngl 99 -fa on -ctk q8_0 -ctv q8_0 -kvu -c 230000 -b 2048 -ub 1024 -npp 4096 -ntg 128 -npl 1,8,16,32,53
```

Then start the worker server with the settings this report describes: 53 slots, a per-slot cap, compressed memory, two checkpoints and a 2 GB prompt cache. This line is untested:

```
llama-server -m <model>.gguf -ngl 99 -np 53 -kvu --kv-unified-per-slot 4608 -fa on -ctk q8_0 -ctv q8_0 -b 2048 -ub 1024 -ctxcp 2 -cram 2048
```

Use the server's built-in JSON-schema output for the final answer ([llama.cpp server README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md)). Put the page snapshot at the end of each prompt, so the unchanged instructions at the start can be reused.

## Conclusion

"53 agents at once" measures throughput, not thinking. On one chip, extra slots speed up writing but not reading, so a page that is half as long does more good than twice as many brains. That reverses the usual instinct to add capacity: the cheapest 53-agent system reads less, and skips the AI entirely when a layout repeats. The three free sources also differ in shape. The laptop gives a steady stream with no end, the free cloud a short, wide burst, and Kaggle a scheduled weekly boost. A good design gives each one the job its shape suits, not the hope that any of them alone is a fleet.

The models small enough to fit 53 slots are the same ones the evidence says fail at open-ended browsing and succeed at filling small forms. That is exactly what rtrvr's demo asked of its agents. A free alternative can beat rtrvr where it matters to you: private by default, no daily cap, no ads. It has to be honest about the split, though. Small local brains fill forms, one smarter planner makes the plan, and plain code does the clicking. The open question is your own hardware. No one has published a test of 53 conversations on a 6 GB laptop GPU, or of the newest hybrid models in llama.cpp at that scale. The benchmark command above answers that, and its result decides which model you run.
