# Better eyes and checkers beat bigger brains

The quickest way to make your fleet smarter is to change what each small brain sees and to check its answers with plain code. Swapping in a bigger brain comes later. Your 0% rating score is a seeing problem. The rating lives in a hidden label inside the page code (`class="star-rating Three"`), your text snapshot throws that label away, and so no brain, however clever, can find it. A brain smaller than yours (0.6 billion numbers inside it, against your 2 billion) scored **88 out of 100** on a standard shop-page test once pages were trimmed down and those labels kept ([AXE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01838)). The next cheapest wins all happen outside the brain. Give each answer a form with fixed choices. Check in code that every answer really appears on the page. Have Gemini write a "recipe" (saved page addresses) once for each kind of page, and accept it only after it gives the right answers on sibling pages, so code can then replay it for free. That last step is also how the fleet learns new sites on its own. On teamwork the evidence is blunt: let brains vote and let tests pick winners, but never let them argue. Debate pulled 7–8B models below plain voting, as low as **65.5% against 76.9%** ([Debate or Vote](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v1)). To need you less, set the "ask the human" line from your own past answers (a robot study cut help requests **10–24%** this way) and keep a memory so no question is asked twice ([KnowNo](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01928v2)). To plan a whole job from one prompt, spend one Gemini call on a fixed plan, let code hand the work out to all 53 brains, and do a 3-page trial run first. Bigger brains come last. Sharper weights fit in memory you already have spare. A fine-tune of the 2B fits too, but the fleet goes offline for hours while it trains. A fleet of 4B brains would shrink from 53 to about 19. Nearly all of this evidence comes from models bigger than yours, so measure each step on your own pages and on sites the fleet has never seen.

## Twelve changes, ranked by gain for the cost

The ranking weighs two things: how strong the measured evidence is, and how cheap the change is on your exact laptop. Costs are counted in **slots**. A slot is one brain, and at 4,096 tokens each one uses **52.8 MiB** of graphics-card memory. A token is a word-piece, about three-quarters of a word. About **5,800 MiB is usable** under Windows, and your 53 brains use 4,426 MiB, which leaves room for about 26 more. Your benchmark also showed that total speed stops rising at about 53 brains (**2.66 pages a second**), so spare memory is better spent on sharper brains than on more of them ([Errand benchmark](Maximum%20parallel%20brains%20on%20laptop%20GPU.md)). "F1" below is a 0–100 score that goes up when a system finds the right answers and down when it gives wrong ones.

Some popular ideas should be skipped because the evidence goes against them. Debate rounds lower small-model accuracy (see the teamwork section). "Thinking mode" for the workers collapsed one 4B model's extraction score from 0.411 to 0.075 ([ContractEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03080)). A mixed fleet of 45 × 2B plus 1 × 4B needs about 7,170 MiB, which does not fit (calculated from the [Qwen3.5-4B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B) and your [benchmark](Maximum%20parallel%20brains%20on%20laptop%20GPU.md)). Speculative decoding, a speed trick, currently needs llama.cpp to run a single stream, `--parallel 1` ([mer.vin](https://mer.vin/2026/05/run-qwen-3-6-mtp-in-llama-cpp-faster-local-inference-with-built-in-speculative-decoding/?amp=1)). Squeezing the memory cache to 4 bits would add slots that bring no extra speed. Libraries of skills meant to carry over to other sites are reused less than 9% of the time on new ones ([PolySkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15863)). Before changing anything, freeze a scoreboard: your 105 cached pages plus 5–10 sites the fleet has never touched, with answers you have checked by hand. Tricks that shine on known sites often shrink or reverse on new ones. Remembered workflows moved whole-task success on unseen websites from 2.0% to 1.6% ([AWM](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07429v1)), and self-training overfits after its first round ([ReST-EM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06585)).

## The 0% rating is a seeing problem, not a thinking problem

A web page has two layers. One is the words you see. The other is hidden labels in the page code that tell the browser how to draw things. On books.toscrape.com the star rating exists only as a label, `star-rating Three`. Your text snapshot keeps the words and drops the labels. Every brain therefore gets a page with no rating on it, and even 53 votes are all wrong together. The same failure shows up in vendor tests with much bigger models. Markdown input caught only **6 of 24** facts that lived only in labels ([Stagehand, vendor](https://www.stagehand.dev/blog/html-vs-markdown-vs-accessibility-tree-llm)). Markdown returned an empty product code when the code sat only in a `data-sku` label ([ScrapingAnt, vendor](https://scrapingant.com/blog/html-markdown-plain-text-llm-extraction)). Those tests used frontier models, but the lesson carries over to yours: a brain cannot read what it is never shown.

The best-measured fix trims the page hard but keeps the structure and labels of what is left. AXE (February 2026) cut shop pages from **16,582 tokens to 351 (97.9% smaller)**. It kept each remaining piece's labels and its exact address in the page, and its 0.6-billion-number Qwen3 model scored **88.10 F1** on SWDE, a standard test of 80 real websites. That beat models trained specially for the task, and on book sites it reached 92.42 ([AXE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01838)). An older study shows the same pattern for question answering. Raw page code scored 44.00, plain text 59.75, and pages trimmed to 4,000 tokens 68.50, or 71.75 with an 8B model ([HtmlRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02959)). One caution: HtmlRAG's cleaning step deletes long labels, which would delete your rating too, so your keep-list has to name the labels to keep. A simple flat list of "address → text and labels" gave the best record-extraction score (0.9567 F1) and the fewest mix-ups about where things sit ([NEXT-EVAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17125)). That suits a 4,096-token slot well. The brain gets a short list it can quote from, and code can check every quote.

Concretely, keep `class`, `data-*`, `title`, `alt`, `aria-label`, `itemprop`, `content` and `href` on the parts you keep, or write the label into the text as `[class: star-rating Three]`. It costs no GPU time at all. Your 70% title score probably has the same cause. Listing pages cut long titles short with "...", while the full title most likely sits in the link's `title` label and on the book's own page. That is an untested guess, so check it. Also add a rule that rejects any value ending in "..." when a longer version exists. Before waking any brain, check whether the page carries a hidden data card. In the 2024 Web Data Commons crawl, **51.25% of sampled pages carried structured data**, and 70% of the sites that add it use the JSON-LD format ([Web Data Commons](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/dws/news/wdc-json-ld-microdata-rdfa-data-corpus-2024-published)). When a page has one, a parser reads it with no AI at all.

Next, give every answer a form. llama-server accepts a "JSON schema", a form with named blanks and rules for each blank, through `response_format` ([llama.cpp grammars](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/grammars/README.md)). You already get 0 broken answers, so the gain now is making wrong answers impossible. Rating must be one of five words, price must match a money pattern, availability must come from a short list. On an 8B model, forms like this nudged accuracy from 80.1 to 82.4 on a maths test, and the form-building step took about 0.05 seconds ([JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868)). Two details matter. Put a short "evidence" blank before the answer blank, because reasoning that comes first helps ([dottxt](https://blog.dottxt.ai/say-what-you-mean.html)). And always allow "not found", or the form will push the brain to invent a believable fake. Keep forms flat. llama.cpp handled only 39% of the hardest real-world forms in that benchmark ([JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868)).

Keep thinking mode off for the workers, as you already do. On clause extraction, Qwen3-4B scored **0.411 with thinking off and 0.075 with it on** ([ContractEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03080)). In NuMind's tests Qwen3.5-4B spent an average of **27,177 thinking tokens** per document and failed 229 outputs ([NuExtract](https://github.com/numindai/nuextract)). That is more than six times your whole 4,096-token slot. The Qwen3.5-2B card itself warns that this size tends to get stuck in thinking loops ([Qwen3.5-2B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B)). One free speed fix is worth making now: your benchmark found that `--reasoning-budget 0` quietly turns off word-picking on the GPU, and that `-rea off` plus `-bs` should give back about 10–15% speed. That setting is still experimental ([Errand benchmark](Maximum%20parallel%20brains%20on%20laptop%20GPU.md)).

## Recipes tested on sibling pages let the fleet learn new sites alone

The cheapest checker is code that confirms every answer appears on the page, ideally by having the brain report the address it read from and then re-reading that address. AXE calls this grounding, and removing it dropped its score from **88.37 to 83.95** ([AXE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01838)). It catches made-up values and cut-off values at almost no cost.

A recipe is a saved address (a "selector") for each field, which code replays on every page of the same kind. Yours got 3 of 5 fields right. The research says small brains are poor recipe authors. On 80 websites and 32,000 pages, the share of fully correct scrapers was **71.56% for GPT-4-Turbo, 54.84% for GPT-3.5 and 24.06% for Phi-3-medium**, a 14B model seven times your brain's size. The authors conclude that small models struggle to write working page addresses ([AutoScraper](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12753v2)). So Gemini should write recipes, and your brains should test them. Testing across pages pays off. Building the rule from 3 sample pages instead of one raised GPT-4-Turbo from 65.31% to 71.56% and GPT-3.5 from 44.52% to 54.84% ([AutoScraper](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12753v2)). The free Crawl4AI library already works this way. Its schema generator tests the selectors against the page and fixes them automatically when they return nothing. It accepts several sample pages, can read labels (for example `"attribute": "href"`), and works with local models ([Crawl4AI docs](https://docs.crawl4ai.com/extraction/no-llm-strategies/)). SCRIBES (ICLR 2026) used exactly this sibling-page check to train script writers and got **over 13% better scripts** ([SCRIBES](https://openreview.net/forum?id=gQSnEIA3Z3)).

The loop for Errand uses parts you already have. First, the brains extract 3–5 sample pages of one kind. Fields like price, stock and product code are already at 100%, so these "silver answers" can be trusted. Second, Gemini writes the selectors, and it may read labels and do small conversions such as "Three" to 3. Third, code replays the selectors on the samples, and a field is accepted only if it matches on every sample. Any failing field goes back to Gemini once, with the failing piece of page, the expected value and the bad selector. Keep one or two samples hidden from Gemini and use them only for testing, so a recipe that fits by luck gets caught. Your two wrong recipe fields are probably rating and title, the same label-only fields, and this check would have flagged both automatically. That is an inference: your logs were not inspected.

To know when a page is "the same kind", group pages by their address pattern (such as `/catalogue/{name}/index.html`) and by a fingerprint of their tag layout. A new fingerprint triggers a new recipe. Then guard the recipes. Old wrapper-repair research compared the style of extracted values (length, character types) against history and beat ordinary regression testing on 27 sites ([Kushmerick 1999](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/1999/kushmerick1999aaai-regression)). A later version caught **35 of 37 real site changes** that broke a recipe ([Lerman, Minton, Knoblock](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.4872)). Add one or two "canary" pages per site, with answers you have confirmed, that every new or repaired recipe must reproduce exactly. If a recipe suddenly returns empty values, identical values or prices ten times higher, mark it stale and send those pages back to the brains.

This per-kind recipe cache is the memory that actually carries over to new sites. Libraries of general skills mostly do not. On unseen websites, one skill system's skills were reused under 9% of the time and another's under 3% ([PolySkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15863)). With GPT-4.1, a plain agent with no skills (56.2%) beat a skill library (54.9%) on new websites. Only libraries that kept updating while they worked on the new site pulled ahead, to 61.3% ([SkillMigrator](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17645v1)). Note that those systems look skills up by page layout, which is what your fingerprint does.

## Fifty-three brains should vote and test, never argue

The clearest small-model study of teamwork found that plain voting explains almost all the gains that had been credited to brains debating. For Qwen2.5-7B, averaged over 7 tests, one brain scored 72.05%, a majority vote 76.91%, and debate 65.5–73.8%. On arithmetic, voting reached **99.0% while debate ranged from 43% to 84%**. Llama-3.1-8B followed the same pattern, and more debate rounds generally made it worse. The authors proved that talking things over does not raise a brain's average chance of being right. It only shuffles opinions around ([Debate or Vote](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v1)). Brains switch from right answers to wrong ones after reading a peer's reasoning, even when stronger models outnumber weaker ones ([Talk Isn't Always Cheap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05396)). Across 16 small judges from 0.6B to 14B, debate lowered accuracy in every setup tried ([SLMJury](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07810)). Your brains are smaller than any of these, so expect the copying effect to be at least as strong. When brains must see each other's work, show them only pass/fail test results, never another brain's reasoning.

Tests beat votes by a wide margin. When unit tests picked the winner, one model went from solving 15.9% of real coding tasks with one try to **56% with 250 tries**, while voting levelled off after about a hundred ([Large Language Monkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787v3)). The math behind it is simple to state. When the right answer leads only narrowly, voting needs far more tries than testing does ([arXiv 2506.05295](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05295)). Your huddle (3, then 9, then 53 brains each proposing a fix, tested on 5 saved pages) already has the right shape. Two upgrades come from the code-writing research. Keep some saved pages for testing only. And raise your trust when several different selectors pull identical values from those test pages. That cross-agreement added 18.8 points in a coding study ([CodeT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10397v2)).

Where there is no test, as with "which of these two prices is the real one?", voting has limits. Most of its gain comes in the first handful of samples. Stopping once the leader is clear used 3.3 times fewer samples on average, at under 0.1% accuracy loss ([Adaptive-Consistency](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11860)). Worse, on hard questions where the brain's most common answer is wrong, more votes lock in the wrong answer ([Are More LLM Calls All You Need?](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02419v2)). A big but split vote among 53 brains proves nothing. Spend extra tries only where something failed. A 2026 method that adds effort only on uncertain steps beat plain voting by up to 11.8% while using fewer tokens ([CATTS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12276)). This matters on your laptop because the GPU is already maxed out at 53 brains, so voting three times on every page would cut your page rate about threefold. Your 25 browser windows also run out before the GPU does. Re-reading a saved snapshot needs no window, so do retries there. When a live retry is needed, several short attempts beat one long one: three 30-step runs scored 86.2%, one 100-step run 78.2% ([MolmoWeb](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08516v1)).

Gemini fits best as a referee inside a "cheap first, escalate on disagreement" ladder. It should not join the discussion, because weaker debaters drag stronger ones down ([Talk Isn't Always Cheap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05396)). Asking GPT-4 only when cheaper answers disagreed matched GPT-4's accuracy at **about 40% of its cost** ([MoT cascade](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03094)). Agreement-based ladders cut cost 2–25 times against other ladders ([ABC](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.02348v4)), and a learned ladder matched GPT-4 at up to 98% lower cost ([FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)). For Errand that means calling Gemini only when no candidate passes the tests, or when two pass but disagree on the hidden test pages. Send it only the survivors and their test outputs. Ask for a short verdict, since 10-token verdicts did as well as long reasoning for small judges ([SLMJury](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07810)). Gemini's pick must still pass the tests.

## A measured ask-line and a memory make you needed less

To ask you less, the fleet needs a reliable warning light. Asking a small brain how confident it feels had no support in anything the research found. Agreement among independent answers is the strongest signal. Grouping answers by meaning and measuring how spread out they are detected made-up answers with an AUROC of **0.78–0.81** across model families ([Nature 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0)). AUROC is a 0.5-to-1 score of how well a warning light separates right answers from wrong ones; 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is perfect. A free second signal is how sure the brain was of each word it wrote. Single-pass signals like this ranked below agreement in a large comparison, but they cost nothing extra ([LM-Polygraph](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.15627)). Meta's DeepConf found that the brain's least sure 10% of words separated right answers from wrong better than its average sureness ([DeepConf](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/deep-think-with-confidence/)). llama-server's own `/completion` endpoint returns word probabilities through `n_probs` ([llama.cpp server docs](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ping98k/llama-cpp-server-doc-qa/viewer/default/train?p=12)). Measure this on the answer words only, such as the price digits or the selector text.

Where to draw the line can be calculated, not guessed. KnowNo turned a model's probabilities into a set of plausible options and asked a human only when more than one stayed in the set. It hit the success level the user asked for while cutting human help **10–24%** ([KnowNo](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.01928v2)). DeepMind's version calibrates a "don't answer" line from agreement scores and keeps made-up answers under a chosen rate ([Conformal abstention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01563v1)). Here is the plain recipe. Log 100–300 past cases where you gave the true answer, along with the fleet's score on each. Then pick the lowest line at which the worst-case error rate among auto-accepted answers stays under your target, say 2%. Everything below the line comes to you. Fixed lines go stale as sites and tasks change ([arXiv 2502.06884](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2502.06884)), so recalibrate whenever a site's layout changes. Keep paying, sending, posting, logging in and CAPTCHAs on the always-ask list. Those are rules, not confidence questions.

So that no question is asked twice, store every answer you give. MemPrompt kept a growing memory of past misunderstandings and the user's corrections, pulled up similar ones for new questions, and stopped repeating mistakes with no retraining ([MemPrompt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.06009)). Agent Workflow Memory turned past successes into reusable step lists and gained **51.1% relative success on WebArena** and 24.6% on a cross-task test ([AWM](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07429v1)). For Errand, each record holds the site, the page-kind fingerprint, the field, the kind of problem, your answer and the date. Before any huddle, look for an exact match on site and field, then a close match on fingerprint. Treat a stored fix like any other huddle candidate: it must pass the page tests before it is trusted. When you give the same kind of answer on two sites, ask once whether it should become a general rule, such as "price lives in the element marked `itemprop=price`". When a site's health checks drift, expire its memories, because an old answer about a changed layout causes silent errors. No study has measured how much such a memory cuts the asking rate in scraping, so your own log will be the first measurement.

## One Gemini call can turn a prompt into a whole job

Planning once and then running the plan beats deciding each step on the fly. LLMCompiler has a planner write a graph of tasks that are then run in parallel. Compared with a step-by-step agent it was **up to 3.7x faster, 6.7x cheaper and about 9% more accurate** ([LLMCompiler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04511)). ReWOO planned with no peeking, used 5 times fewer tokens, and showed that a 7B model could learn the planner's job ([ReWOO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18323v1)). A May 2026 study on web tasks found that a plan written once at the start generally beat replanning at every step. The step-by-step agents looped, lost track of progress, or marked unfinished subtasks as done ([arXiv 2605.29927](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29927)). The best planner-plus-worker result, 53.94% on a web test, replanned after every single step with fine-tuned 70B models ([Plan-and-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09572v2)). That would take one Gemini call per step, which a free key cannot pay for.

So the planner's contract is simple. One Gemini call turns your prompt into a structured plan with four parts: the fields wanted, each with its type and check (price is a number with a currency sign, stock is in/out/limited); the list of addresses, or a step that finds them from a sitemap or a search; a graph of steps (fetch, extract, compare); and the shape of the final output. A job like "compare prices on 3 shops" also needs a final matching step, done in code, so no single brain has to hold all three shops in mind. Code then groups the addresses by kind of page, runs a 3-page trial for each kind, makes one Gemini repair call if a trial fails, and only then hands the work out to all 53 brains. That costs about one Gemini call per job plus one per new kind of page. No study tests "trial run on 3 pages" as a gate by itself; SCRIBES's sibling-page check is the closest evidence ([SCRIBES](https://openreview.net/forum?id=gQSnEIA3Z3)). Free-tier daily limits are uncertain, and third-party sources disagree, with figures like 100–1,000 requests a day for Flash-Lite ([tinkerllm](https://tinkerllm.com/blog/gemini-api-free-tier-limits-rate-quotas/), [agentdeals](https://agentdeals.dev/gemini-api-pricing-changes)). Check Google's own page before relying on any number.

Jobs that need clicking, searching or filtering on a new site are harder, and you should discount the headline scores. A simple agent solved 51% of tasks on the older WebVoyager test but only 22% on the stricter Online-Mind2Web. Popular agent frameworks added little on top of the brain inside them: Browser Use scored 30.0% and SeeAct 30.7%, both with GPT-4o ([Illusion of Progress](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01382v4)). The common failures were ignoring requirements, inventing constraints, repeating actions and stopping early. **Failed runs took about twice as many steps as successful ones**, so an unusually high step count makes a cheap early-warning alarm ([same study](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01382v4)). Trained small agents can do far better. A 1.7B text agent went from 11.5% to **56.9% on 3,000 websites it had never seen**, beating the 235B model that collected its training data ([InSTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06776v2)). A 4B agent reached 67.0% on Online-Mind2Web ([OpenWebRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02031)), above the 61.3% scored by OpenAI's Operator in 2025. But InSTA's data took 1,200 graphics-card-hours to collect, and OpenWebRL's training took about 300 top-end GPU-hours plus $545.5 of judge calls per run, so neither recipe is $0 at that scale. Until then, keep each worker's step tiny and concrete, and write the checks into the plan rather than trusting the brain to remember them. No study has yet paired a frontier planner with a 1–4B web worker, so this split is a reasoned bet, not a measured result.

## Bigger or retrained brains come last, and each costs memory or downtime

Your spare 26 slots are best spent making each brain sharper. Model weights can be stored with fewer digits per number to save memory, which is called quantization. Q4 means about 4 bits per number and Q8 about 8, and more bits means a sharper brain that uses more memory. In a Qwen3 study, the 1.7B model lost **6–7 points** on a knowledge test at 4 bits and nothing at 8 bits, and smaller models lost more ([Qwen3 quantization](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02214)). GGUF files with an importance matrix (Unsloth's UD quants) lose less than that study's methods ([Unsloth KLD tables](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.5/gguf-benchmarks)), so expect a gain of a few points, not six. Scaling from the 4B's file sizes ([Unsloth Qwen3.5-4B GGUF](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen3.5-4B-GGUF)), Q6_K costs about 6 slots and Q8_0 about 14. Both fit while keeping all 53 brains. Measure the change with `llama-perplexity --kl-divergence` and on your 105 pages. A second zero-memory trick is to paste 1–2 already-solved examples from the same site into the prompt. Studies found no gain beyond 5 ([arXiv 2402.03832](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.03832)), and adding one known-wrong example helps ([C-ICL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11254)). Two examples of about 600 tokens use about 15% of a slot.

Fine-tuning gives the brain extra lessons on one job and saves them as a small add-on file (a "LoRA"). A full-precision LoRA of Qwen3.5-2B needs **about 5 GB**, so it fits only with the fleet stopped. The 4B needs about 10 GB, and Unsloth advises against its memory-saving QLoRA mode for Qwen3.5 ([Unsloth Qwen3.5](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.5/fine-tune)). Unsloth installs natively on Windows. Reinforcement-style training needs vLLM, which does not run on native Windows, so that would need WSL ([Unsloth Windows](https://unsloth.ai/docs/get-started/install/windows-installation)). A rough calculation gives **35–60 minutes per pass over 1,000 examples**, so 1–3 hours overnight. Measure one pass first, because laptop heat can slow it down. The finished brain exports to the same Q4_K_M size, so it costs **0 slots**. A mismatched chat template is the most common reason an exported model performs worse ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.5/fine-tune)). The payoff is well documented: 310 small fine-tunes beat GPT-4 by **10 points on average** and their own base models by 34 ([LoRA Land](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00732v1)).

Train only on rows your checks have proven: the recipe replay agrees with the brain and the forms pass. That is the "keep only answers a checker accepts" method, which gained **5.94 points** for PaLM 2-S on maths. Most of the gain came in the first round, and later rounds got worse on coding tests, so stop after one or two rounds and judge on unseen sites ([ReST-EM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06585)). Choose examples carefully: 1,000 hand-picked examples worked, while random picks scored about 30% worse ([s1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.19393)). Favor hard, varied cases that were wrong and then fixed. Be careful with Gemini outputs as training data. Google's terms say you may not use the service to "develop models that compete with the Services", which is undefined for a small extraction add-on. Training on its Search- or Maps-grounded results is banned outright, and on the free tier human reviewers may read what you send ([Gemini API Terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). The lowest-risk pattern is for Gemini to judge and measure while your own verified rows do the teaching. This is not legal advice. Qwen3.5 itself is Apache 2.0, so fine-tunes of it are unrestricted ([Qwen3.5-2B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B)).

## Conclusion

The 53 brains are best thought of as a cheap idea generator, and its ceiling is set by the checkers around it: the labels it is shown, the forms it fills, the sibling pages and canary pages its recipes must pass. Upgrading a checker lifts every brain at once, for free. Upgrading the brain lifts only the brain and costs slots. There is a second shift as well. Once recipes replay in code, the graphics card stops being the scarce resource. Browser windows, Gemini calls and your attention become the things to ration, and they belong on new kinds of pages and real disputes, not on routine ones.

Every human answer and every verified row can be used three times: as a point that sets the ask-line, as a memory entry that stops a repeat question, and as a lesson for the next fine-tune of the 2B. That lets the fleet get smarter by running, at $0, in a way that downloading a bigger model cannot. The biggest unknown is that nobody has published a test of a roughly 2B brain doing this exact work, so the ranking above is a bet backed by evidence from larger models. The scoreboard of never-seen sites is what turns it into knowledge.
