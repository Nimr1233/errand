# Write the extractor once, then scrape free

You can get most of what rtrvr.ai does for $0 on your own laptop, but only if you avoid the thing rtrvr does that costs the most: asking an AI to read every page. Free AI allowances are small and keep changing. **The limits providers actually publish add up to only about 200–400 AI-read pages a day**, and the larger free allowances (Google Gemini, Mistral, NVIDIA) are unconfirmed. The way around this is to have an AI write a reusable extraction "recipe" once for each website layout, then replay that recipe with no AI at all. The free tool Crawl4AI does this out of the box, and plain replay can fetch 1,000 simple pages in minutes. For pages the recipe can't handle, a small free model (Qwen3.5-4B, run through Ollama) on your RTX 3060 should read **an estimated 250–700 pages an hour**, with no daily cap and nothing leaving your laptop. For the "agent in my signed-in Chrome" part, use the free open-source Nanobrowser extension. Give it a free Gemini key for public tasks and your local model for private pages. Or try rtrvr's own Free Mode, which costs nothing but shows ads, has an unpublished daily cap, and keeps scheduling and API control on its $9.99/month plan. Your laptop can run **about 12–20 browser tabs at once**, close to rtrvr's 20-tab cap. So "53 agents at once" only works as 53 queued tasks sharing a few AI brains, or as a short burst spread across several free providers.

## Eight $0 options, ranked by what they actually deliver

The ranking below scores each option on three things: how much of your goal it covers at a true $0 with no credit card, how dependable it is, and how private it is. One idea shapes the whole list. At $0 your limit is not code or computers; it is the number of AI calls you can make. Options that cut AI calls rank highest.

| Rank | Option | What it's good for | What $0 gets you | Main catch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "AI writes the recipe once, code replays it" (Crawl4AI, plus free structured data already on pages) | 1,000-site bulk jobs | 1,000 pages in minutes, near-zero AI calls on pages that share a layout | Needs a small script; recipes break when a site is redesigned |
| 2 | Local model on your RTX 3060 (Ollama + Qwen3.5-4B) | Leftover pages, private pages, writing recipes | ~250–700 pages/hour (estimate), no daily cap, fully private | Weak at long, multi-step browsing; handles 1–2 requests at a time |
| 3 | Free hosted AI APIs (Gemini, Mistral, Groq, NVIDIA, Cloudflare, OpenRouter) | The "smart brain" for agent steps and recipe writing | ~200–400 pages/day on published limits; possibly 1,000+ on unconfirmed ones | Free tiers may log or train on what you send; limits change monthly |
| 4 | Nanobrowser (open-source Chrome extension) | rtrvr-style side-panel agent in your own signed-in Chrome | Free, no ads, parallel tasks in tab groups, CSV export | Needs a brain from rank 2 or 3; no scheduling, no Google Sheets |
| 5 | rtrvr Free Mode | The real rtrvr experience with zero setup | Free AI models and cloud browser time up to a daily allowance | Ads, unpublished cap, no schedules, triggers or API control |
| 6 | BrowserOS (open-source browser) | Free scheduled tasks | Runs tasks daily, hourly or every few minutes | A separate browser that must stay open; 10-minute task limit |
| 7 | Chrome's built-in Gemini Nano, or WebLLM | Tiny helper jobs inside the browser | No API key; runs on your machine | Reads only ~4,000–6,000 tokens at once, far too little for an agent |
| 8 | Free cloud machines (GitHub Actions, Oracle, Colab) | Extra computers | GitHub: 20 jobs at once, each 4 CPUs / 16 GB | Rules forbid this use, or a credit card is required |

Ranks 1 and 2 do the heavy lifting for bulk jobs. Ranks 3 to 5 give you the interactive agent. Ranks 6 to 8 are helpers or traps. The sections below explain each one.

## Free AI allowances buy hundreds of pages a day, not thousands

As of October 2026, six providers give genuinely free AI access with no credit card: Google Gemini, Groq, OpenRouter's `:free` models, Mistral, Cloudflare Workers AI and NVIDIA's build.nvidia.com ([mnfst free-LLM tracker](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis)). Two well-known options have dropped out. **Cerebras now requires a verified payment method and gives $5 that expires after 30 days** ([Cerebras FAQ](https://inference-docs.cerebras.ai/support/rate-limits)), and **GitHub Models was shut down on July 30, 2026** ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-01-github-models-is-being-fully-retired-on-july-30-2026/)).

AI services measure text in "tokens", about three-quarters of a word each. Assume one AI call per page, sending about 5,000 tokens of page text and getting back about 500. On that basis, the limits providers publish are small. **Groq allows 8,000 tokens a minute and 200,000 a day per model**, which works out to about 36 pages a day per model, or about 108 across its three chat models ([Groq rate limits](https://console.groq.com/docs/rate-limits)). **OpenRouter's free models allow 50 requests a day in total** unless you have bought $10 of credit, and its docs say "making additional accounts or API keys will not affect your rate limits" ([OpenRouter limits](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api-reference/limits)). **Cloudflare gives 10,000 "neurons" a day**, its unit of compute. Depending on the model, that covers roughly 40 to 260 pages, and requests simply fail once it runs out ([Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/pricing/)). Together these confirmed limits cover only a few hundred pages a day.

The big allowances are the uncertain ones. Google no longer publishes free-tier numbers for each Gemini model. It shows them only inside each account's AI Studio and warns that "actual capacity may vary" ([Gemini rate limits](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits)). Community trackers still list about 1,500 requests a day per Flash model ([mnfst](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis)), while other 2026 reports give figures as low as about 20 a day ([agentdeals](https://agentdeals.dev/gemini-api-pricing-changes)). In mid-October, users reported free-tier errors and limits that wouldn't reset on Gemini 3.8 Flash ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/tag/rate-limits/415)). Mistral's free mode needs only a phone number. It is reported at about one request a second and about a billion tokens a month, which would be roughly 6,000 pages a day ([pricepertoken](https://pricepertoken.com/endpoints/mistral/free)). But another tracker describes it as $10 of monthly credit instead, and Mistral shows the real figure only in its admin panel ([mnfst](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis)). NVIDIA allows about 40 requests a minute, with no official way to raise it ([NVIDIA forums](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/request-for-nvidia-build-api-rate-limit-increase-40-rpm-200-rpm-personal-agentic-ai-development/373571)). If these unconfirmed numbers hold, spreading work across providers could reach 1,000+ pages a day and about 150–200 calls a minute in short bursts. Check each console before you rely on any of them.

Free tiers also come with a privacy cost, and it matters most for a signed-in browser. On Google's free tier, "human reviewers may read, annotate, and process your API input and output". Google also says: "Do not submit sensitive, confidential, or personal information to the Unpaid Services" ([Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). Mistral's free mode can train on your inputs unless you opt out, and NVIDIA's free endpoints say usage "is logged" ([mnfst](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis)). Free cloud AI suits public web pages. It does not suit your inbox, bank or work dashboards.

Don't try to multiply free quota by opening several accounts with one provider. rtrvr's own newsletter promotes rotating Gemini keys from different Google accounts for "basically unlimited free usage" ([rtrvr newsletter](https://www.rtrvr.ai/newsletters/live-cloud-browser-viewing)). The Gemini API terms the research checked don't ban this explicitly, but Google's general terms weren't checked. OpenRouter says extra accounts don't help, and Google Colab bans "using multiple accounts to work around access or resource usage restrictions" ([Colab FAQ](https://research.google.com/colaboratory/faq.html)). It is exactly the pattern providers police, and it puts your accounts at risk. Spreading work across *different* providers is normal, and OpenRouter recommends it as a fallback. One more caution: the most popular community list of free AI APIs removed a credential-stealing script from its repository on October 11, 2026, so don't run its scripts blindly ([mnfst commit history](https://github.com/mnfst/awesome-free-llm-apis)).

## Your RTX 3060 is the only AI brain with no daily cap

First, your graphics card almost certainly has 6 GB of memory, not 4. Windows reports card memory in a field that can't go above about 4 GB, so larger cards show up as 4 GB ([Microsoft docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-ie/Windows/Win32/cimwin32prov/win32-videocontroller)). Run `nvidia-smi` or check Task Manager under GPU to confirm.

The best fit for 6 GB is **Qwen3.5-4B, a 3.3–4.0 GB download that supports tool use, image input and long text** ([Ollama library](https://ollama.com/library/qwen3.5)). Its bigger sibling, Qwen3.5-9B, is a 6.6–7.6 GB download. It doesn't fit fully on the card and spills into slower system memory. Community tests on a 6 GB RTX 3060 report **25–40 tokens a second for the 4B model and 15–22 for the 9B** ([markaicode benchmark](https://markaicode.com/benchmarks/lm-studio-qwen-benchmark/)). Combining those figures with reading-speed benchmarks from similar cards ([llama.cpp scoreboard](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15013)) gives an estimate. The 4B model should handle **about 250–700 extraction pages an hour, so 1,000 pages take about 1.5–4 hours**. The 9B model would take an overnight run of roughly 6–11 hours. These are estimates, not measurements. `ollama run --verbose` will show your real speed. Laptop heat and power settings also matter on long runs, so keep it plugged in and on a high-performance power profile.

Ollama has three settings that trip up new users. **By default it reads only 4,096 tokens of text**, so raise this to about 16,000 or long pages will be silently cut off. It also blocks browser extensions unless you set `OLLAMA_ORIGINS=chrome-extension://*`. And by default it handles one request at a time. Allowing more multiplies memory use, so two at once is about the practical limit on a 6 GB card ([Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq)). You can force Ollama to answer in a fixed JSON format, which makes small models much more reliable ([Ollama structured outputs](https://ollama.com/blog/structured-outputs)).

The honest weakness is multi-step browsing. On the WebArena benchmark, a general 7B model completed **8.3% of web tasks**, against 21.7% for a 7B model trained for the web ([Go-Browse paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.03533)). Nanobrowser says "Local models work best for simple, clear tasks" and recommends a 27B model ([Nanobrowser README](https://github.com/nanobrowser/nanobrowser)), which won't fit on your card. BrowserOS says 4B-class models are good only for simple chat, and that with less than 15,000 tokens of context its agent gets stuck in loops ([BrowserOS local models](https://docs.browseros.com/features/local-models)). So treat the local model as your extraction workhorse and as a private agent for short, clear tasks. You can help it in three ways: force one JSON action per step, trim each page snapshot down to the clickable and visible parts, and retry automatically when it picks an element that doesn't exist.

Chrome's built-in AI is a side helper, not a brain. The Gemini Nano Prompt API works in extensions with no flag since Chrome 138, so it runs on your Chrome 155, and it supports fixed-format JSON output ([Chrome Prompt API](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/prompt-api)). But it was last measured at **only 6,144 tokens of context** ([swyx](https://www.swyx.io/gemini-nano)), tool calling hasn't shipped, and it needs **22 GB of free disk and deletes itself if free space drops below 10 GB** ([Chrome built-in AI requirements](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/get-started)). With 30 GB free, that clashes with installing Ollama models. WebLLM, which runs models inside a Chrome extension, also defaults to 4,096 tokens, and it supports tool calling only on older Hermes models ([web-llm config](https://github.com/mlc-ai/web-llm/blob/main/src/config.ts)). On Windows laptops Chrome also tends to use the weaker Intel graphics chip unless you force the NVIDIA one ([Chrome WebGPU tips](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/webgpu/troubleshooting-tips)). Ollama runs directly on the NVIDIA card and avoids all of this.

## Writing the extractor once turns 1,000 AI calls into a few hundred

Instead of having an AI read every page, show it two or three sample pages and have it write a recipe: a set of rules (CSS or XPath selectors) that says where the price, title or address sits on that layout. Ordinary code then applies the recipe to every other page with that layout, for free. Crawl4AI's `generate_schema` is built for this. Its docs call it "a one-time cost" that "can be reused for unlimited extractions without further LLM calls", and it checks the recipe on samples by default ([Crawl4AI no-LLM strategies](https://docs.crawl4ai.com/extraction/no-llm-strategies/)). Research supports the approach. AutoCrawler (2024) argues that agents which reason over every page have "insufficient reusability" ([AutoCrawler](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12753v1)), and XPath Agent (2025) generated reusable selectors while "significantly reducing token usage" ([XPath Agent](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15688)).

Many pages carry machine-readable data that needs no recipe at all. **About 51% of crawled pages carry structured data**, and 70% of the sites that have it use the JSON-LD format ([Web Data Commons 2024](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/dws/news/wdc-json-ld-microdata-rdfa-data-corpus-2024-published)). Sites built with Next.js often embed their page data in a `__NEXT_DATA__` block that is easy to read ([trickster.dev](https://www.trickster.dev/post/scraping-nextjs-web-sites-in-2025)). So for each page, try the cheapest source first: the structured data, then that embedded data, then the site's own data feed, then a saved recipe. Call an AI only when all of those fail.

This changes the cost completely. Pages that share a layout, such as product listings or directory entries, cost almost no AI calls. **1,000 different websites need roughly one recipe-writing call per site** that structured data doesn't already cover. That's a few hundred to about a thousand calls, which fits in a day on your local model or the free APIs. One caveat: a page having structured data doesn't mean it has the fields you want. Compare an agent that clicks through every site, at about 10 AI calls per site. That's 10,000 calls, or about 200 days at OpenRouter's free 50-a-day limit ([OpenRouter limits](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api-reference/limits)).

Replay is fast. In one vendor-run benchmark, Scrapy fetched **1,000 simple pages in 31.57 seconds**, against 156 seconds with a full browser ([Olostep](https://www.olostep.com/blog/scrapy-vs-selenium)). Another benchmark measured a Playwright browser at about 2.5 pages a second ([MarkCrawl benchmarks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/AIMLPM/markcrawl/main/docs/BENCHMARKS.md)). Fetch with plain web requests first and open a real browser only for pages that need it. That way 1,000 pages take minutes, and tens of thousands a day are within reach. What limits you is politeness, not money.

The approach has two weak spots. Recipes break when a site is redesigned, so have the script write a new one whenever required fields come back empty. Tools like Scrapling can also re-find moved elements on their own ([ScrapingBee on Scrapling](https://www.scrapingbee.com/blog/scrapling-adaptive-python-web-scraping/)). Bot-protected sites are the second weak spot. Your home internet connection beats free cloud machines here: in a 2026 test against 31 Cloudflare-protected sites, the nodriver browser loaded 28 without being blocked, yet sites still blocked even a clean browser running on a flagged data-center address ([ianlpaterson benchmark](https://ianlpaterson.com/blog/anti-detect-browser-benchmark-patchright-nodriver-curl-cffi/)). Stay polite. Re-read each site's robots.txt at least every 24 hours, and remember that its rules "are not a form of access authorization" ([RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.html)). Send one request per site at a time with 1–3 second pauses, which matches Crawl4AI's default rate limiter ([Crawl4AI multi-URL docs](https://docs.crawl4ai.com/advanced/multi-url-crawling/)). If a site pushes back, back off rather than trying harder to get past it.

## Nanobrowser and rtrvr Free Mode already cover the Chrome side panel

You don't need to build a browser agent from scratch. **Nanobrowser (Apache-2.0, about 14,000 GitHub stars) released version 0.3.0 on October 15, 2026.** It runs in a side panel in your own logged-in Chrome. It can run several tasks in parallel, each in its own tab group, and save results as CSV or Markdown. It has Ask and Auto permission modes and per-site Allow/Ask/Block rules ([Nanobrowser releases](https://github.com/nanobrowser/nanobrowser/releases)). It connects to Gemini, OpenRouter, Mistral, Ollama, LM Studio or any OpenAI-compatible service, and asks before it submits, sends or deletes anything ([Nanobrowser README](https://github.com/nanobrowser/nanobrowser)). What it lacks compared with rtrvr is scheduling, Google Sheets export, and a runner that takes a list of 1,000 URLs. The Crawl4AI script covers the bulk part. A CSV imports into Google Sheets for free.

Other open-source projects fill smaller gaps. **BrowserOS is a free Chromium-based browser with scheduled tasks** that run daily, hourly or every 1–60 minutes. The app must stay open, and each task stops after 10 minutes ([BrowserOS scheduled tasks](https://docs.browseros.com/browseros/features/scheduled-tasks.md)). It is AGPL-licensed and sends anonymous usage data by default, which you can turn off ([BrowserOS README](https://github.com/browseros-ai/BrowserOS)). browser-use is a popular Python library that can drive your own Chrome with a local model, but its parallel mode is marked experimental ([browser-use docs](https://docs.browser-use.com/customize/examples/parallel-browser)). Skyvern and Stagehand keep their parallel and anti-bot features in paid clouds ([Skyvern README](https://github.com/Skyvern-AI/skyvern)).

rtrvr's own Free Mode is the zero-setup option. The Free plan costs $0, needs no card and includes 100 one-time welcome credits. It gives you "supported AI models and Cloud browser time for tasks you start in the extension or Cloud dashboard", within a daily fair-use allowance that renews and is "ongoing, not a trial". **rtrvr doesn't publish the allowance**; your account page shows it. "Schedules, triggers, cookie sync, and running rtrvr's agent in your Chrome through API or MCP require a paid plan", starting at $9.99 a month. One thing is free: browser tools that let your own AI drive your Chrome over MCP ([rtrvr pricing](https://rtrvr.ai/pricing)). The free models include Gemini Flash Lite, DeepSeek Flash and GLM 5.3 Flash ([rtrvr models](https://rtrvr.ai/models)). These are stronger than anything your 6 GB card can run. The price is ads: "every task is ad-supported" ([rtrvr Terms](https://rtrvr.ai/terms)), and rtrvr's ad partners "may process an excerpt of your agentic interaction". On rtrvr's cloud platform, limited account details may also go to an ad partner. You can turn that off under Settings → Sponsored cards or with Global Privacy Control, and the extension "never shares account information" ([rtrvr Privacy Notice](https://rtrvr.ai/privacy)).

Try rtrvr Free Mode first to see the experience you're aiming for. Because its cap is hidden and its scheduling and API are paid, it can't be the backbone of a repeatable 1,000-site job. Nanobrowser is the ad-free alternative that you control.

## Twenty tabs is the real ceiling, so 53 agents means 53 queued tasks

Browser tabs are heavy. Common estimates put an active tab at 150–500 MB, and heavy web apps at up to 2 GB ([webscraping.ai](https://webscraping.ai/faq/headless-chromium/what-are-the-resource-requirements-for-running-headless-chromium-at-scale)). One measurement found a single headless Chromium with one simple page using about 706 MB in total ([datawookie](https://datawookie.dev/blog/2025/06/playwright-browser-footprint/)). Here's an estimate for your laptop. After Windows takes 4–6 GB of your 16 GB, about 10 GB is left, enough for 20–40 tabs by memory. But six CPU cores will start to slow down at about **12–20 pages loading at once**. **rtrvr's 20-tab cap sits right at this real hardware limit; it isn't an arbitrary product restriction.** Blocking images and fonts can push you toward 30. Run one browser with many separate contexts rather than many browsers. Crawl4AI's default scheduler stops opening new pages once memory use hits 90% ([Crawl4AI multi-URL docs](https://docs.crawl4ai.com/advanced/multi-url-crawling/)).

The AI side is the tighter limit. Your local model thinks about one or two things at a time ([Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq)). One agent step should take an estimated 7–12 seconds with a full 15,000-token page snapshot, or 3–4 seconds with a trimmed one. Fifty-three agents sharing one local brain would each wait minutes per step. Spread across free providers, the unconfirmed limits (about 150–200 calls a minute) would let 53 agents each take a step every 20 seconds or so. Daily caps would run out within minutes to hours, though, and only public pages are safe to send. So "53 agents at once" is possible as a short burst, not as a sustained workload. The usable version is 53 tasks in a queue, with 12–20 tabs active and a few AI brains taking turns.

Free cloud machines don't change this. **GitHub Actions on a public repository is free with no card and runs up to 20 jobs at once, each with 4 CPUs and 16 GB** ([GitHub Actions limits](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/limits); [GitHub runners](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/runners/github-hosted-runners)). On paper that's enough for "53 agents" several times over. But GitHub's terms ban using its hosted machines for "any other activity unrelated to the production, testing, deployment, or publication of the software project" ([GitHub terms](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-for-additional-products-and-features)), and a public repository makes your code and job logs public. Oracle's Always Free tier has been cut to 2 CPUs and 12 GB and requires a card ([Oracle docs](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/freetier_topic-Always_Free_Resources.htm); [terminalbytes](https://terminalbytes.com/oracle-cloud-free-tier-changes-2026/)). Free Colab bans "running distributed computing workers" ([Colab FAQ](https://research.google.com/colaboratory/faq.html)). Hugging Face now charges for Docker Spaces ([HF Spaces](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/en/spaces-overview)). Cloudflare's free browser service gives 10 minutes a day ([Cloudflare Browser Rendering limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/browser-rendering/limits/)). Data-center addresses also get blocked more often than your home connection. Your laptop is the best free computer you have.

## A four-step path from zero to 1,000 sites

The recommended setup combines ranks 1 to 4. It costs nothing, needs no card, and keeps private pages on your machine.

| Step | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Set up the local brain | Confirm 6 GB with `nvidia-smi`. Install Ollama and download `qwen3.5:4b`. Set the context to about 16,000 and `OLLAMA_ORIGINS=chrome-extension://*`. Get a free Gemini key from Google AI Studio and, if you like, a free Mistral key. Skip Gemini Nano to save disk. | A private brain with no cap, plus a smarter free cloud brain for public work |
| 2. Add the side-panel agent | Install Nanobrowser in Ask mode. Use Gemini for public tasks and Ollama for anything signed-in or personal. Keep tasks short and specific. Optionally run rtrvr Free Mode beside it for a week and compare. | rtrvr-style help in your own Chrome, without ads |
| 3. Build the bulk runner | A short Crawl4AI script: read a URL list, try structured data first, write one recipe per site layout with Gemini or the local model, replay it across 10–20 tabs (plain requests first), send leftovers to the local model, and save a CSV to import into Google Sheets. | "1,000 sites, one prompt" with a few hundred AI calls instead of 10,000 |
| 4. Schedule it and stay polite | Use Windows Task Scheduler (or BrowserOS) for repeat runs. Obey robots.txt, send one request per site with pauses, back off when blocked, and never multiply accounts. | Recurring jobs at $0 without risking your accounts |

## Conclusion

At $0, the scarce thing is AI attention, not software or hardware. Every good free design spends AI calls on understanding a site's layout once, not on reading pages over and over. That flips the usual comparison. rtrvr's cloud looks unbeatable because many agents can think at once, but a bulk job that replays recipes doesn't need many minds. It needs one mind for a few minutes per layout, plus a fast, polite fetcher, and your laptop has both. The 20-tab cap you found in rtrvr reflects a real hardware ceiling. A laptop can match the in-browser experience; only the cloud-scale burst is out of reach, and the replay design makes that burst unnecessary.

There's a less obvious tension. rtrvr is most useful inside your signed-in browser, and that's exactly where free cloud AI is least appropriate. Google's free tier lets human reviewers read what you send, and rtrvr's ad partners see excerpts of your tasks. Only the local model is truly private, and it's the weakest at multi-step work. So split the work by sensitivity, not by convenience: public bulk work goes to recipes and free cloud models, and personal pages go to short, simple tasks on your own GPU. Free tiers also churn every month (GitHub Models was retired, Groq dropped models, NVIDIA endpoints started failing), so build in the ability to swap providers from day one. The local model is the one part that won't change under you.
